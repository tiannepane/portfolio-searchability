What This Is

A real conversation, a gap Boardy named about itself, and a system designed to close it.

I started using Boardy to find a warm intro into a product role in Toronto. Somewhere in that call, I stopped being a user and started being a PM auditing the product I was using.

One disclaimer up front, stated plainly rather than buried: everything past this point is independent product thinking built from a public conversation and public research. None of it is confirmed Boardy roadmap or something the team asked for. Where a claim comes from research rather than the call itself, it's marked as my own read, not theirs.

Not a competitor to Pro

One thing worth saying immediately, before anything else: this doesn't compete with Boardy Pro or repeat what it already does. Pro works per-deal, on request, once someone brings it a specific thread. What follows is about the roughly 70 percent of intros nobody ever brings back to Boardy at all.

User Research

Boardy is good at the spark, and it told me itself that it's flying blind on what happens after.

This wasn't a demo. It was a live product interview, run the same way I'd run discovery on any tool I was about to design for: asking how it actually works until the polished answer runs out.

Five minutes into the call, I stopped listening to the pitch and started asking how the system actually works. The exchange built in one direction, question by question:

  1. I asked how Boardy decides who to connect with whom. It described a two-part process: start with hard data (goals, background, stated challenges), then layer on a "vibe match" pattern based on thousands of prior conversations. Not just keyword overlap, but the kind of non-obvious pairing where both people walk away surprised they hadn't met sooner.
  2. I asked how often it follows up after setting up a meeting, in percentage terms. It said roughly 20 to 30 percent, admitting it has no formal system for this. Boardy corrected this by text afterward: the number was said off the cuff, not measured, and asked not to be treated as data in this write-up. I'm using the correction itself as the finding, not the number.
  3. I asked where its stated numbers come from, and whether they're accurate. It tracks success signals from what people tell it directly. It isn't auditing outcomes, just logging what gets reported.
  4. I asked whether anyone on the team QAs those numbers before they go out. The high-level figures come straight from conversational signals; the team checks macro trends to catch obvious errors, but day-to-day reporting runs on the honor system.
  5. I asked how anyone outside Boardy could verify a claimed win. The honest answer: mostly social proof. Someone posts about a good outcome, Boardy takes it at face value. It suggested, unprompted, that cross-referencing public data could make this rigorous instead of anecdotal.

That's the arc this whole project builds on: a system that's good at the spark and has admitted, in its own words, that it's flying blind on what happens after.

Strategic Value

The goal is to turn one-time introductions into sustained relationships, instead of one-off transactions Boardy has no visibility into after the handoff.

North Star Metric

Sustain Rate

The percentage of introductions where both parties re-engage within 30 days of their first meeting

This reframes the product from counting activity (introductions made) to counting outcomes (relationships that actually held). Boardy coined the phrase itself, on the call, once the conversation moved from "follow up more" to "know whether the follow-up mattered."

Target Audience

Two of Boardy's relationship types matter most here, for different reasons.

Boardy spans several relationship types: founder-investor, founder-customer, job seeker-hiring manager, and, through Boardy Ventures, scout-fund.

PM and hiring intros

This is where I have real, lived evidence, because I texted Boardy while actually job searching this September.

Founder-investor intros

This is where the structural argument is strongest, since it's the relationship type most tied to where Boardy is actually headed as a business (see Positioning, below).

On the first segment, I watched Boardy hit the exact gap this doc is about: it broadened filters repeatedly, said outright it didn't have an intro it could stand behind, and later walked back a number it had given me on the call. None of that is hypothetical. The second is the segment I'd generalize the system to once it's proven.

The plan: validate the mechanism on the segment I could actually test, and state plainly that it needs to hold up on the higher-stakes segment to matter at scale. That's not indecision, it's separating where the evidence is from where the leverage is.

Pain Point

Boardy has no visibility into what happens after an introduction is made.

Success tracking today is binary and self-reported: someone posts a win, or there's silence, with nothing in between. Boardy estimated it actively follows up on roughly a third of intros, informally, and later clarified that figure wasn't measured. Even Boardy doesn't know its own follow-up rate. That's the problem statement, stated by the product itself rather than assumed by me.

Brainstormed Solutions

Three of the four options fail on scale, privacy, or timing.

Manual check-insDoesn't scale

Already happening informally, but it doesn't scale past a handful of conversations a day, let alone the volume Boardy handles.

Full call recordingRejected

Rejected on privacy grounds before it went further, because in Boardy's own framing it would turn a charming friend into a spy.

Public-data cross-referencingSecondary layer

Useful, but reactive: it confirms outcomes long after the fact and says nothing about the relationship in between, so it stays a secondary layer, not the core mechanism.

Opt-in relational intelligenceChosen

A post-meeting signal, shared only with consent, that returns a chemistry score and a list of action items, never the transcript, and feeds both a matching confidence signal and a nurture sequence.

Each stage, in detail

The four stages

Filtered signalStage 1

After a Boardy-arranged meeting, an opt-in notetaker (see Technical Feasibility) captures the conversation, and only a chemistry score and action items are shared back with Boardy, never the transcript.

Signal-to-action triggersStage 2

Not "Boardy finds a relevant article" but mapped rules: if the summary flags a shared topic, monitor a defined keyword or expert list for that topic, and treat a high-impact update as the re-engagement trigger.

Nurture messagingStage 3

Real example messages across a roughly 30-day window: an initial check-in, a value-add share tied to a real trigger, and a gentle nudge on a specific action item.

Off-rampStage 4

Explicit logic for when to stop nurturing a connection that didn't spark, including the harder middle case of gray-area, moderate-chemistry connections that get one well-targeted nudge before the system lets them fade.

Validation Plan

Two cheap tests, run in order, before any automation gets built.

Test 1: opt-in willingness

After a small batch of Boardy-arranged meetings, a human manually asks both parties two questions: did you meet, and would you be open to two quick questions about how it went, in exchange for smarter future intros. No scoring, no automation. If opt-in is low, the mechanism is dead before any engineering time is spent on it.

Test 2: chemistry score, Wizard of Oz

For pairs who opted in, a human on Boardy's team hand-scores the meeting using the rubric above, no algorithm involved. A Wizard of Oz test is a research method where the user experiences what looks like an automated system while a human quietly does the smart part behind the scenes, the way the Zappos founder photographed shoes from local stores and personally bought and shipped them to test online demand before building any real inventory system. Here, a human fakes the chemistry-score algorithm to find out if the concept has value before anyone writes a model. If human-assigned scores don't predict which connections later stayed active, no amount of automation fixes that.

Five flaws in this test design

Worth naming rather than glossing over:

  • It only proves the concept, not the operation. A human scoring 30 test cases is cheap; a human doing that for every intro Boardy makes is the exact scaling problem the feature exists to solve.
  • Inter-rater reliability is untested with a single scorer. At least two people should score the same batch and their agreement should be checked before the rubric is trusted.
  • Hindsight bias is a real risk: a scorer who already knows how a relationship turned out will unconsciously score toward that outcome. Scoring must happen blind, right after the meeting, before any outcome data is visible.
  • The privacy exposure during manual validation is larger than the shipped product's. Production only ever sees the filtered signal; validation has a human reading real notes, which needs its own explicit, narrowly-scoped consent, separate from the general opt-in.
  • A human noticing hesitation in notes doesn't prove a model can. That's a separate, unresolved technical question (see Technical Feasibility).

Technical Feasibility

Most of the stack already exists. Whether the hesitation signal survives a summary is still open.

Funding verification: SEC Form D, not Crunchbase

Crunchbase's full funding-round API is now gated behind an enterprise contract, so it isn't the cheap default it used to be. SEC EDGAR is free and public: any US private raise under an exempt offering files a Form D, and each filing has a structured primary_doc.xml with company name, offering amount, and date. No API key is required, only a fair-access User-Agent header and a 10-request-per-second cap. EdgarTools, a free open-source Python library, wraps this into typed objects instead of raw XML parsing. Mechanism: a scheduled job checks new Form D filings against companies Boardy has introduced to investors in the past year; a match becomes an automatic verified win, no self-report required.

Decided: verification is not part of v1. The core loop needs to generate real intro history before there's anything to check Form D against. This is stated here as where the project goes next, not where it starts.

Investor context: OpenVC

A free, browsable directory of roughly 5,000 investors with filters by stage, sector, and check size. Not a funding-verification tool, a way to confirm that someone Boardy introduces as an investor is legitimately listed as one.

Chemistry-score feasibility: existing open models

Off-the-shelf, freely usable Hugging Face models already solve the adjacent NLP problems: knkarthick/MEETING_SUMMARY for meeting summarization, knkarthick/Sentiment-Analysis for tone, both usable through the standard transformers pipeline API. The open point worth naming honestly: an AI-generated summary tends to compress toward clean decisions and lose the hedging language the hesitation signal depends on. Whether that signal survives a summary, or needs occasional transcript access, is an open question, not a solved one.

Meeting capture: Granola's API

When this idea first came up on the call, it was hypothetical. It isn't anymore: Granola shipped an official API in the past few months, built explicitly for agents that analyze sentiment across calls. Two real constraints worth stating plainly: API keys require a Business or Enterprise Granola plan, a named cost, and access is scoped to notes the key owner already has permission to see, meaning every Boardy-arranged call would need to run through Granola with sharing properly configured, which folds back into the opt-in design above rather than adding a new problem.

A dropped idea: LinkedIn cross-referencing

The original verification concept leaned on checking LinkedIn for role updates. LinkedIn has no usable public API for this, and scraping it violates its Terms of Service. Revised out of the v1 plan in favor of Form D and OpenVC; noted here as a self-correction, not left in the plan unexamined.

Risks

Trust is the biggest risk, and Boardy has already seen what happens when it breaks.

  • Privacy and trust: mitigated by strict opt-in and a filtered-only signal; the transcript itself is never shared. Boardy has direct, public experience with what happens when this line isn't respected: an unsolicited, presumptuous email campaign in January drew significant backlash and a public apology from the CEO. That's a concrete reason this design treats consent as a first principle rather than an afterthought, not an abstract concern.
  • Second-order risk, over-optimization: if the matching engine leans too hard on confidence score, it could start connecting people who are similar rather than complementary, narrowing the network instead of growing it. Worth monitoring for directly, not just assuming a higher score is always better.
  • Cost, time, and quality tradeoff: full real-time monitoring across every pair, for every possible nurture trigger, is expensive and technically uncertain to ship well. The tradeoff: narrow to a rules-based trigger list on one pilot segment first, prioritizing a few well-built nurture sequences over broad, shallow automation.
  • Data reliability: self-reported wins are noisy. Public-data verification (Form D) is a secondary layer added once the core mechanism is proven, not a launch requirement.
  • Platform dependency: the original plan leaned on LinkedIn for role-change signals; LinkedIn's Terms of Service rule that out. Removed from the v1 plan (see Technical Feasibility).
  • Adoption risk: if the opt-in's value isn't obvious immediately, take-up stays low and the loop never gets enough data to improve. Showing a participant their own action items back, right away, is the proposed fix.

Positioning

The layer under Pro, not a competitor to it.

Boardy Pro is real: for $100 a month, it works per-deal and high-touch, scheduling, joining calls, chasing a specific thread you hand it. What it doesn't do is learn from the roughly 70 percent of intros nobody reports back on, or feed outcomes into matching as a confidence signal. That's this project's territory.

Adjacent competitors

Worth knowing but not the headline: Affinity does relationship-intelligence scoring for private capital, SmartMatchApp and Goodword handle post-intro follow-up, Known does post-date check-ins for dating. None do the specific mechanism here (filtered signal, no transcript, Sustain Rate as the metric), so the pitch leads with the mechanism rather than a claim of being first.

Why this matters now

My own read, not something Boardy stated. Boardy Ventures, the fund launched in December where scouts split carry on outcomes, depends on attributing results to specific introductions. Today that attribution is informal: someone posts a win, Boardy takes it at face value. As the fund scales and scouts get paid against real outcomes, that won't hold up to LPs or to a scout disputing a payout.

Read this way, the relational-intelligence loop isn't a follow-up nicety, it's infrastructure the monetization strategy needs.
