The Journey

"How do we get multiple vendor quotes in front of a property manager before the next phone call?"

That gap was the one thing standing between us and a signed contract, so we needed a working solution, integrated into their existing systems, fast. It went through three versions.

V1: Doing It Manually

Before any software touched this, property managers called whoever they already knew, one at a time, and waited to see who called back first.

V2: n8n

I built the first working version in n8n, the fastest way to get something live and wired into their systems that week. Given a broken-down building and a trade, it found nearby vendors and fired off outreach. It worked well enough to close the deal.

But "nearby" was doing too much work: no cap on distance, no check on licensing or trade match. It ran for two days, with small errors piling up the whole time, before it queued outreach to vendors two hours outside a building's service area, in the wrong trade entirely. Trust eroded fast, on both sides.

V3: LangChain

Once real customers were depending on it, I rebuilt it as a proper multi-agent system:

  • An orchestrator that routes the issue
  • A vendor-matching agent that checks known, trusted vendors near that building first, pulled from a list tagged by trade, urgency, price, and availability
  • A vendor-sourcing agent that only searches for new vendors when there isn't a known match
  • An outreach agent that reaches each one through whatever channel gets the fastest response (there are services now for automating phone calls; we looked at it and chose not to)
  • A quote-aggregation agent that normalizes what comes back
  • A follow-up agent chasing anyone who goes quiet

No quote reaches the property manager, and no vendor gets hired, without a person approving it.

Reflection

Don't ship a matching rule without a checkable boundary

"Nearby" almost cost us a vendor relationship two hours outside where we actually needed one. Every rule since gets a specific, testable limit before it ships.

Five agents means five places to fail quietly

Five independent LangChain agents can each fail quietly, one at a time, with nothing upstream noticing. We built Grafana dashboards tracking each agent's latency and completion rate individually, so a stall points straight to the agent that's stuck.
